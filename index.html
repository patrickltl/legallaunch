Guide

How to generate a Privacy Policy — and the other legal pages your website actually needs

If you launched a website, an app, or a small SaaS, you’ve been told you need legal pages. Here is what each one is for, what regulators and platforms expect to see in them, and how a privacy policy generator like this one produces all four in minutes.

Why every website needs a privacy policy

A privacy policy is the document that tells visitors what personal data you collect, why you collect it, who you share it with, and what rights they have over it. It is the one legal page with truly global reach: the EU’s GDPR and the UK GDPR apply to any site that can be reached from Europe, and the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) attaches to eligible businesses serving Californians. The obligations follow your visitors, not your headcount — a weekend blog using Google Analytics needs a policy just as much as a funded startup.

Practical enforcement comes from platforms, too. Google Analytics’ terms require you to post a privacy policy that discloses its use. Ad networks and affiliate programs condition approval on one. Both the Apple App Store and Google Play reject submissions that lack a linked privacy policy. In short: if you want analytics, ads, affiliates, or app-store distribution, a privacy policy isn’t optional.

What a good privacy policy contains

Regulators and app reviewers look for a recognizable structure: who operates the site and how to contact them; the categories of data collected (contact details, account data, payment metadata, automatically collected technical data); the purposes for using it; the cookies and trackers involved; third-party services that receive data; how long you keep it; how you secure it; and how users can exercise their rights. If you serve EU or UK visitors, the policy should also state a lawful basis for each processing purpose under GDPR Article 6 and describe data subject rights under Articles 15–21 — access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability, and objection — plus safeguards for transfers outside the EEA, such as Standard Contractual Clauses. For California, expect a CCPA section covering the right to know, the right to delete, the right to correct, the right to opt out of sale or sharing, and the promise of non-discrimination, with a 45-day response window. A quality terms and conditions generator or privacy policy generator should produce all of this conditionally — clauses that appear only when they actually apply to you.

Terms and conditions: the quiet workhorse

Terms of Service set the contract between you and your users: what your service does, who may use it, what’s prohibited, who owns the content and the code, and — critically — your disclaimers and limitation of liability. The liability cap and the “as is” warranty disclaimer are what keep a small bug or outage from becoming an existential claim. Your terms should also name your governing law and venue, explain how the terms can change, and cover account termination. If you sell anything, the terms should hand off to a refund policy rather than contradict it.

Cookie policy: small document, big signal

Under the EU ePrivacy rules, non-essential cookies require consent before they run — which is why EU visitors see cookie banners everywhere. The banner needs somewhere to point: a cookie policy that lists each cookie by name, provider, purpose, and lifetime. The honest way to produce that table is to derive it from the services you actually use. A cookie policy generator that knows you run Google Analytics adds _ga (2 years), _gid (24 hours), and _gat (1 minute); a Meta Pixel adds _fbp; Stripe adds its strictly necessary fraud-prevention cookies. A generated inventory that matches reality beats a generic list every time.

Refund policy: fewer chargebacks, more trust

A refund policy is optional in the strictest sense and essential in practice. Payment processors ask for one during underwriting, customers look for it before buying, and a clear policy meaningfully reduces chargebacks — because a cardholder who knows how to get their money back doesn’t need to dispute the charge instead. Decide your window (14-day and 30-day money-back guarantees are the common patterns; digital-only sellers often choose all-sales-final with a defect guarantee), explain exactly how to request a refund and how long it takes to appear, and if you serve EU or UK customers, include the digital-content withdrawal waiver wording: buyers who request immediate delivery and acknowledge it lose the statutory 14-day right of withdrawal. Saying this plainly in your refund policy generator output protects both sides.

Template vs. subscription vs. lawyer

A lawyer is the right choice when your situation is genuinely complicated — health data, financial services, children’s products, or an active dispute. A subscription service ($10–20 per month, indefinitely) rents you templates that stay online only while you keep paying. A one-time generator sits between: it asks the questions a lawyer would ask, writes full-length documents from your answers, gives you files you own outright, and lets you regenerate when your practices change. It is not legal advice — no template is — but for the overwhelming majority of websites, a thorough, honest, well-structured set of policies that you actually keep updated beats a perfect draft that never gets written.

FAQ

Honest answers to real questions

Is this legal advice?

No. LegalLaunch is a self-help software tool that produces document templates based on the answers you give. It is not a law firm, and nothing it produces is legal advice, legal review, or a substitute for a licensed attorney. The documents are a strong starting point that cover the sections regulators, app stores and ad networks typically expect — but only a qualified lawyer in your jurisdiction can advise on your specific situation. If you handle sensitive data, operate in a regulated industry, or face a legal dispute, talk to a lawyer.

Are the generated policies actually valid?

They are professionally structured templates that include the sections commonly required by GDPR, UK GDPR, CCPA, app store guidelines and advertising networks — numbered clauses, effective dates, contact details, and conditional GDPR or CCPA sections based on your answers. Their legal effect depends on your answers being accurate and your practices matching what the documents say. Keep them updated when your site changes; that is true of every template, including ones from lawyers.

Do I really need a privacy policy for a small website?

In practice, yes. GDPR applies to any site visited by people in the EU, and CCPA applies to eligible businesses serving Californians — the obligations follow your visitors, not your size. Google Analytics’ terms require you to post a privacy policy, as do most ad networks and affiliate programs, and both the Apple App Store and Google Play reject app submissions without a linked privacy policy. A short, honest policy beats no policy.

What changes when I toggle GDPR or CCPA on?

Turning on EU/UK adds a lawful-basis section citing GDPR Article 6, data subject rights citing Articles 15–21, international transfer safeguards (Standard Contractual Clauses), and the right to complain to a supervisory authority. Turning on California adds a CCPA section: the right to know, delete and correct, the right to opt out of sale or sharing, non-discrimination protection, and the 45-day response window under Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1798.100–125. The cookie policy also switches to consent-first language for EU/UK visitors.

Can I edit the documents after downloading?

Yes. You get clean, watermark-free files in HTML, Markdown and plain text with no restrictions on your own content — edit them in any editor, paste into your CMS, or restyle the HTML to match your brand. There is also an in-app Edit mode so you can tweak wording and re-download. You can re-generate documents from your saved answers any time.

Why a one-time payment instead of a subscription?

Because a legal page is a document, not a service that gets more expensive to host every month. Subscription legal-page tools charge roughly $10–20 every month for years. LegalLaunch is $29 once: you keep the files forever, there is no account, and the generator runs in your browser so you can re-generate whenever your site changes.

Do I need a cookie policy and a cookie banner?

If you have visitors from the EU or UK and use anything beyond strictly necessary cookies — analytics, advertising pixels, embedded videos — you must get consent before those cookies run, and you must tell people what you use. A cookie policy that lists each cookie, its provider, purpose and lifetime is the standard way to do that. The generator builds that table automatically from the services you select. The banner itself can be any consent-management script; the policy is what it links to.

Where do my answers and documents go? Do you store them?

Everything runs in your browser. Your answers are saved to your browser’s local storage so a draft survives a refresh, and the documents are generated on your device with JavaScript — there is no server that receives them, no account, and no submission of your data to us.
